Data and calculations ＋

One measure, consistent units

Annual greenhouse gases (GHG), total excluding LULUCF. The file specifies kilograms CO₂-equivalent per person with a multiplier of 10³. We display tonnes per person: raw value × 1,000 kg ÷ 1,000 kg/t. Numeric values are unchanged.

Comparisons you can reproduce

Change = (end ÷ start − 1) × 100. Indexed values = year ÷ selected start × 100. If the start value is unavailable, the index is unavailable. Absolute and indexed trend axes start at zero. No missing values are filled or carried forward.

Coverage and aggregation

The export includes 89 countries/territories and five regional aggregates. Aggregates are excluded from country rankings. The OECD line is the published aggregate, not an average of the countries selected here. A per-person decline does not establish a decline in total emissions.

Limits to the claim

This data does not establish why emissions changed. Reporting years differ. Forty of the 792 observations are marked estimated. All forty belong to the OECD, OECD America, OECD Asia Oceania and OECD Europe aggregates. The filtered export has no sector or individual-gas breakdown.

Why the original chart is misleading ＋

Selected countries

Estonia, the United Kingdom, Luxembourg, Greece, Germany and Spain are the six OECD member countries with the largest per-person reductions between 2015 and 2020 in this export. An equal-weight average of their indexed series makes this exceptional group look representative. It gives Estonia the same influence as Germany.

Time range and scale

The briefing ends in 2020, so the steepest year is the last one shown and the rise in 2021 is left out. Continuing to 2024 increases the decline to 30.6%. The index axis begins at 70, making a roughly one-quarter decline occupy most of the plot. The ticks are real, but the visual emphasis is selective. The lab isolates the scale effect without changing a single value.

Title and labels

“The transition is working” implies a cause that this dataset cannot establish. “Lower emissions” blurs the distinction between per-person emissions and totals. The descriptive wording control replaces the headline and labels with a neutral statement.

Even a repaired chart has limits. The OECD aggregate is not the world. Every OECD aggregate value in this export is marked estimated. Neither series proves a policy effect or a fall in total emissions.

Write-ups

White hat write-up

Dataset and question. We used the OECD greenhouse gas inventories to ask: how have greenhouse gas emissions per person changed since 2015, and how do countries compare? Our export contains one measure, total greenhouse gases per person, excluding land use, land-use change and forestry. It has 792 observations from 2015 to 2024, covering 89 countries and territories and 5 regional aggregates. It does not cover the 1990 to 2018 range listed in the assignment, and it has no breakdown by sector or gas. The OECD aggregate fell 13.6% between 2015 and 2024, from 11.92 to 10.29 tonnes per person. Of the 32 OECD members with 2024 data, 31 fell. In 2022, the United States was at 18.57 tonnes and the United Kingdom at 5.96.

Data transformations. The file reports thousands of kilograms per person, which we display as tonnes without changing the numbers. We calculate percent change and an index where the selected start year equals 100. Rankings exclude aggregates so regions are not ranked against countries. We do not fill missing values, so a country with no start-year value has no index or percent change.

Marks and encodings. We used a line chart because the question is about change over time. Both views use a zero-based axis so the scale does not exaggerate change. The chart opens on tonnes per person because an index starts every country at 100, which hides differences in level. The index is available as a second view for comparing proportional change. Lines are labeled directly. Gaps mark missing years, hollow points mark estimated values, and tooltips give each value and its status. A table supports comparing countries in a single year, with search and sorting. It opens on 2022, when 81 countries have data, compared with 41 in 2024.

Titles and labels. The text under the heading states the data question. The chart title describes what is plotted without stating a conclusion, and the subtitle and axis give the unit. In the index view, the subtitle says values show proportional change, not total emissions. The legend marks aggregates, and the note below the chart explains hollow points and gaps. A marker labels 2020 as the first pandemic year, without claiming a cause.

Limits and possible bias. Coverage changes over time. All 89 countries and territories have data in 2020 and 2021, but only 41 do in 2024, and most non-OECD countries are missing by then. Rankings from different years are therefore not directly comparable. The United States ends in 2022 and China in 2021. All 40 estimated values belong to the four OECD aggregates. The OECD line continues through 2024 even though 6 members have no 2024 value, and the file does not say how those gaps are handled. Holy See has the highest 2022 value at 31.40 tonnes, and the file has no population figures or notes that explain it. Finally, a fall in emissions per person does not prove that total emissions fell, and this data cannot show why emissions changed.

Black hat write-up

Dataset and question. This chart uses the same OECD file and measure as the white hat chart, limited to six countries from 2015 to 2020. It is built to support one answer, not to ask an open question. The chart says emissions fell 23.4% and that the transition is working. Every value is calculated from the OECD file and the arithmetic is correct. The direction is also accurate, since 34 of 38 OECD members fell between 2015 and 2020. The chart misleads about the size of the decline and implies a cause the data cannot show.

Data transformations. We chose the six OECD members with the largest per-person declines from 2015 to 2020: Estonia, the United Kingdom, Luxembourg, Greece, Germany and Spain. The chart names them but does not say how they were chosen. Their average decline is 23.4%, compared with 12.7% for the OECD aggregate and 12.1% for the median member. We averaged their indexed values with equal weight, so Estonia (37.5% decline) counts the same as Germany (20.2% decline). Without Estonia, the average decline is 20.6%.

We also ended the series in 2020. The change from 2019 to 2020 accounts for 12.0 of the 23.4 points. WHO characterized COVID-19 as a pandemic in March 2020, and the chart does not mention this. The data cannot show whether the pandemic caused the drop. Ending in 2020 makes the steepest year the last one shown and leaves out the 3.7 point rise in 2021. It does not inflate the total: continuing to 2024 increases the decline to 30.6%.

Marks and encodings. The index axis starts at 70, so the decline fills 67% of the plot height, compared with 22% on an axis from 0 to 105. The shading under the line stops at 70, which treats that value as the bottom of the chart. The line, shading and large figure are green, which supports the positive headline. The dataset has no emissions targets, so it cannot show whether this decline counts as success.

Titles and labels. The headline says the transition is working, which states a cause, but the dataset contains only one emissions measure and nothing about policy or energy use. The headline also says emissions are falling, in the present tense, but the series ends in 2020. The large label says lower emissions, with per person only in smaller text below it. The dataset has no total emissions, so it cannot support a claim about totals. The legend calls the line a progress index, which presents a measurement as a judgment.

Limits and possible bias. This chart is biased on purpose. We kept the elements readers expect: a labeled axis, named countries, a stated method and a source line. No value is false, so finding the problems requires asking how the countries were selected and comparing them with the OECD aggregate. Separate controls below the chart undo the country selection, the cutoff, the axis and the wording.

Development process

Team. Santosh Sureshkumar focused on data cleaning and the visualizations. Anitej Suklikar focused on styling and the write-ups.

Process. The project includes a script that checks the OECD export and prepares it for the page, and 9 tests that check the calculations.

Time. We spent about 16 people-hours in total. Data cleaning and styling took the longest. Data cleaning involved checking the units, separating regional aggregates from countries, and handling coverage that changes by year. Styling involved simplifying the first design and adjusting the layout for smaller screens.

AI use

Tools. We used Codex and Claude Code.

How we used them. We used both tools to understand and clean the data, to help with development, to check whether our visualization ideas would work with the data, and as a peer reviewer in addition to human review. Codex generated code and compared three designs for showing the black hat choices, which led to the comparison controls. Claude Code drafted these write-ups from our direction, and we reviewed them.

Mistakes we caught. The tools made errors, so we checked their work. An earlier version of the page said the 2020 cutoff hid the later trend, but the data shows the decline grows through 2024. It also said later observations can be estimates, but all 40 estimated values are in the OECD aggregates across every year. Claude Code gave a wrong median in a draft, which a recheck of the CSV corrected.

How we checked. We rechecked figures against the original CSV, ran 9 tests on the calculations, and tested the page in a browser.

Reflection. Codex compared three ways to show the black hat choices: static explanations, a free-form chart editor and guided toggles. It rejected the editor because changing many settings at once makes it hard to see which choice changed a reader's impression. We kept the toggles because they undo one choice at a time using the same data.

The first version looked too polished for what we wanted, so we had it simplified and the slogans removed. We also treated AI output as something to check. Checking the page against the data showed that the claim about the 2020 cutoff was wrong, because the decline grows through 2024 instead of being hidden by the cutoff.

Next time, we would write down reader feedback when we collect it. We showed the chart to other people but did not keep a record of their responses.
